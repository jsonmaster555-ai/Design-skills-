# Content Chunking

Break information into meaningful, manageable units that represent one concept or a closely related set of concepts.

Use headings, spacing, labels, lists, sections, and—only when useful—dividers or containers to expose those units.

Do not create a card for every chunk. Proximity and spacing can often communicate structure with less visual noise.

Avoid both extremes: enormous uninterrupted blocks and interfaces where every sentence becomes a separate visual object.

## Chunk boundaries

A chunk should have a clear beginning, internal relationship, and separation from neighboring concepts. Internal gaps should generally be smaller than the gap to the next chunk.

## Scan anchors

Give important chunks recognizable anchors such as concise headings, labels, key values, or icons when icons genuinely improve recognition.

## Content order

Within a chunk, place the most identifying or decision-relevant information first. Supporting details should follow rather than compete with the chunk's main purpose.

Chunking should improve comprehension and scanning while preserving conceptual continuity.
