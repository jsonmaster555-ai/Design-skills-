# Visual Hierarchy

Visual hierarchy controls what the viewer notices first, second, third, and last. Build hierarchy through size, position, spacing, contrast, typography, color, visual weight, alignment, and isolation.

Do not make every element important. Establish primary, secondary, and tertiary information. If an element needs more prominence, first reduce competition around it before making it enormous or excessively bold.

## White space and proximity

White space is an active structural tool. Related elements should generally sit closer together than unrelated elements. Use larger gaps to separate groups and sections. Do not simply maximize empty space: every gap should communicate a relationship.

## Alignment

Use deliberate shared edges, baselines, columns, and anchors. Small accidental misalignments can make otherwise polished interfaces feel unstable.

## Typography

Use a restrained type scale and limited weights. Prefer Regular, Medium, and Semibold for most UI. Avoid Bold, ExtraBold, and Black by default. Large text already carries visual weight and often needs less weight, not more.

## Contrast and visual weight

Strong contrast attracts attention. Reserve the strongest contrast for information and actions that deserve it. Balance large objects against other visual weight rather than simply centering everything mathematically.

## Corner radius and containers

Use a coherent radius system. Do not make every control pill-shaped. Do not automatically wrap every group in a card. Proximity and spacing can often communicate grouping more quietly.

## Diagnostic

When a design feels wrong, inspect hierarchy, alignment, spacing, scale, typography, contrast, balance, consistency, density, container use, and visual noise before adding decoration.

Good design is intentional relationships.
